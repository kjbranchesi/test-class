# Check an AI draft before you send it

An AI tool can write a clean, confident draft that is still wrong. Once it goes out under your name, every mistake in it is yours.

## What to do

1. **Read it once for the point.** Before you check any details, decide whether the draft says what you need it to say. Write your main point in one sentence of your own, then see whether the draft makes that point in its first few lines. If it does not, fix the order first, because polishing text in the wrong order wastes time.
2. **Check every fact against where it came from.** Names, dates, numbers, prices and quotes are the details most worth checking, because a wrong one can look just as convincing as a right one. Go back to the original for each: the email thread, meeting notes, contract or report you gave the tool. If a fact is not in any of those, delete it or confirm it with the person who owns that information, such as the project lead for deadlines or the finance contact for figures.
3. **Cut anything you did not ask for.** AI tools often add extras: a promise, an apology, an offer of help. Look for any sentence that commits you or your team to something, such as a date, a discount or a next step. Keep it only if you have the authority to make that promise and you mean to keep it.
4. **Read it out loud as the person receiving it.** Tone problems are easy to miss on screen and hard to miss when you hear them. Listen for phrases you would never say, like stiff openings or piled-up praise. Replace them with the words you would use if you were talking to that person face to face.
5. **Check who it goes to and what is attached.** Last, look at the To and CC lines. A draft written as a reply to an old thread may still include people who should not see this message. Open each attachment to confirm it is the version you mean to send, not just a file with the right name.

## A quick example

Jordan, a project coordinator, asks an AI tool to draft a client update about a late shipment and pastes in the team's meeting notes. The draft looks polished. Jordan writes the point in one line: "The shipment moves to March 14, and nothing else changes." The draft buries the new date in its third paragraph, so Jordan moves it to the top. Next, Jordan checks the date against the meeting notes and finds the draft says March 12. Jordan corrects it. The draft also offers a 10 percent credit for the delay, which nobody discussed, so Jordan deletes that line. Reading aloud, Jordan hears "We deeply regret any inconvenience this may have caused" and changes it to "Sorry for the delay." Finally, Jordan sees that the reply still copies a contractor who left the project last month, removes that address and sends the update.

## The mistake people make

The most common mistake is checking only the writing. A draft can be smooth, polite and free of typos and still carry the wrong date or a promise nobody agreed to. Clean writing makes these errors easier to miss, because the text feels finished. Check the point, the facts and the commitments before you spend time on style.

## Quiz

1. An AI draft says a client's contract renews on June 30. You cannot find that date in the email thread, notes or contract you gave the tool. What should you do?
   A. Keep it, since the tool probably drew on accurate information.
   B. Ask the AI tool whether the date is correct.
   C. Add "around" before the date so it is not a firm claim.
   D. Take it out unless the account owner tells you it is right.

2. A draft to a supplier ends with: "We'll have the signed order back to you by Friday." You did not ask for this line. When should it stay in?
   A. When it makes the message sound more helpful.
   B. When you are allowed to commit to Friday and plan to deliver by then.
   C. When the supplier asked for a date in an earlier email.
   D. When every other fact in the draft has been checked.

3. Maya's AI draft is four paragraphs long. Her real request, approval for a budget, appears only in the last paragraph. What should she do first?
   A. Move the request near the top before working on anything else.
   B. Check the budget figures against her spreadsheet.
   C. Read the draft out loud to check the tone.
   D. Shorten each paragraph so the request is easier to find.

4. Reading a draft out loud, you hear the opening "I hope this message finds you well and thriving." What should you do?
   A. Keep it, because formal openings are safe in work email.
   B. Delete the greeting so the message starts with the point.
   C. Change it to how you would actually greet that person in conversation.
   D. Ask the AI tool for a more natural version.

5. Your AI draft has a file attached called "Q3 budget FINAL." What should you do before sending?
   A. Trust the file name, since it is marked final.
   B. Look inside the file to make sure its contents are what the recipient should get.
   C. Rename the file so it matches the subject line.
   D. Ask the AI tool whether it attached the right file.
